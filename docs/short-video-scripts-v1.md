## Script 1 — “Panels are not the whole solar system”

**Hook / on-screen text:**
Most beginners ask: “How many solar panels do I need?”

**Voiceover:**
Most people start with solar panels, but panels are only one part of the system.

Panels answer: how much power can I make during sunlight?

Batteries answer: how long can I run things when the sun is gone?

And the inverter answers: how much power can I use at one time?

That last one matters. A full battery still won’t run a microwave, pump, or refrigerator startup surge if the inverter is too small.

Before you buy a kit, size all three together: panels, battery, and inverter.

## Script 2 — “The inverter mistake”

**Hook / on-screen text:**
This is the solar kit mistake that surprises beginners.

**Voiceover:**
A lot of people size their solar setup around the biggest appliance they own.

But inverter sizing is not just “what is my biggest appliance?”

It’s: what might run at the same time?

Your refrigerator, freezer, lights, Wi-Fi router, fan, and water pump may overlap. Then a compressor or pump starts and creates a surge.

That’s why a system can shut down even when the battery is full.

For beginner planning, add up likely simultaneous running watts, then add startup surge and some safety headroom.

## Script 3 — “Solar generator vs DIY solar kit”

**Hook / on-screen text:**
Should you buy a solar generator or build a DIY solar kit?

**Voiceover:**
Here’s the simple version.

A solar generator — or portable power station — is easier. Battery, inverter, charge controller, outlets: usually all in one box. Great for camping, renters, small backup, or simple plug-and-play power.

A DIY solar kit is more flexible. You choose the panels, batteries, inverter, charge controller, wiring, and safety gear. Better for cabins, RVs, sheds, and systems you may expand later.

The tradeoff is simplicity versus flexibility.

If you want fast and easy, look at portable power stations.

If you want expandability and control, learn the component system.

## Script 4 — “Why a 400-watt panel does not make 400 watts all day”

**Hook / on-screen text:**
A 400W solar panel does NOT make 400 watts all day.

**Voiceover:**
This confuses almost everyone at first.

A 400-watt panel is rated under ideal test conditions. In real life, output changes with sun angle, clouds, heat, shade, dirt, season, and wiring losses.

So you don’t multiply 400 watts by 24 hours.

A rough planning method is: panel watts times peak sun hours, then reduce for real-world losses.

Example: 400 watts times 4.5 peak sun hours is 1,800 watt-hours before losses.

After losses, maybe closer to 1.3 to 1.6 kilowatt-hours per day.

**Visual notes:**

## Script 5 — “Batteries provide time”

**Hook / on-screen text:**
Solar batteries are not about “more power.” They’re about time.

**Voiceover:**
Panels make energy. Batteries store energy. But the easiest way to think about batteries is time.

How long do you want your loads to run when solar is low or the grid is out?

A refrigerator for a few hours is very different from a cabin overnight. And that is very different from whole-home backup for two days.

Battery capacity is usually measured in watt-hours or kilowatt-hours.

But not every bit of battery capacity is usable. You also lose some energy through the inverter and system inefficiencies.

So if your loads need 3 kilowatt-hours, you probably don’t want exactly a 3 kilowatt-hour battery. You need some cushion.

## Script 6 — “12V, 24V, or 48V?”

**Hook / on-screen text:**
When does a 12V solar setup stop making sense?

**Voiceover:**
Small solar systems often use 12 volts. That’s common for RVs, vans, boats, and small DC loads.

But as the inverter gets larger, 12V systems have to move a lot of current.

More current means thicker cables, more heat, and more voltage drop.

That’s why medium systems often move to 24V, and larger off-grid or home backup systems often use 48V.

A simple beginner rule: if you’re looking at a 3,000 watt inverter or larger, don’t automatically assume 12V is the best choice.

At that point, compare 24V and 48V equipment too.

## Script 7 — “The cheap kit problem”

**Hook / on-screen text:**
That cheap solar kit might not include what you think it includes.

**Voiceover:**
When you compare solar kits, don’t only look at panel watts.

A “1,200 watt kit” might sound big, but the battery bank may be too small, the inverter may not handle your surge loads, or the kit may not include the wiring and safety equipment you still need.

Before you compare prices, check four things:

Panel watts.

Usable battery capacity.

Inverter continuous watts and surge watts.

And what is actually included: charge controller, cables, fuses, breakers, disconnects, mounting, and instructions.

A low price only helps if the kit fits the job.

## Script 8 — “Fridge backup reality check”

**Hook / on-screen text:**
Can a solar generator run a refrigerator?

**Voiceover:**
Usually, yes — but you need to check two numbers.

First: running watts. A refrigerator might only use around 100 to 200 watts while running.

Second: startup surge. When the compressor kicks on, it can briefly need much more power.

That surge is what trips up small inverters and small power stations.

Then check battery capacity. If the refrigerator averages 1 to 2 kilowatt-hours per day, a tiny power station may only run it for a few hours.

So the question is not just “can it run a fridge?”

It’s “how long can it run the fridge, and can it handle startup surge?”

## Script 9 — “Beginner solar checklist”

**Hook / on-screen text:**
Before buying solar gear, answer these 5 questions.

**Voiceover:**
Before you buy panels, batteries, or a solar generator, answer these five questions.

One: what are you trying to power?

Two: how many hours per day will those loads run?

Three: which loads have startup surge — like refrigerators, pumps, air conditioners, and tools?

Four: do you need battery backup for hours, overnight, or multiple days?

Five: is this a plug-and-play portable setup, or a component system that needs wiring and safety equipment?

If you can answer those, solar shopping gets a lot less confusing.
