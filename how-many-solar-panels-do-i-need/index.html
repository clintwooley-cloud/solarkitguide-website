Solar panel sizing guide

How Many Solar Panels Do I Need?

The number of solar panels you need depends on how much electricity you use, how much sun your location gets, panel wattage, system losses, and whether you are sizing a full home, cabin, RV, shed, or backup setup.

Quick answer

For a home solar estimate, divide your average daily kWh use by your average peak sun hours, then multiply by a loss factor such as 1.25. Divide that solar array size by the wattage of one panel. For example, a home that needs 40 kWh per day in a 4.5 peak-sun-hour area may need roughly a 11.5 kW array, or about 29 400-watt panels.

The basic solar panel formula

Solar panel count starts with energy, not roof size. First estimate how many kilowatt-hours you want the system to produce on an average day. Then convert that daily target into solar array watts.

Daily kWh ÷ peak sun hours × loss factor = solar array kW
Solar array watts ÷ panel watts = number of panels

The loss factor matters because solar panels rarely produce their lab-rated wattage all day. Heat, inverter losses, wiring, dust, shade, panel angle, weather, and battery charging losses all reduce real production.

Example from an electric bill

If your electric bill shows 1,200 kWh in a month, divide that by 30. That is about 40 kWh per day. If your location averages 4.5 peak sun hours and you use a 1.25 loss factor, the array estimate looks like this:

40 kWh ÷ 4.5 sun hours × 1.25 = 11.1 kW solar array
11,100 watts ÷ 400W panels = about 28 panels

That does not mean every home with a 1,200 kWh month needs exactly 28 panels. It is a planning number. Roof direction, shade, utility rules, net metering, seasonality, and whether you want 50%, 75%, or 100% bill offset can change the final design.

Example for a cabin, RV, or shed

Off-grid systems should usually be sized from appliance loads instead of a monthly utility bill. Add up running watts, hours per day, and duty cycle for each appliance to estimate daily watt-hours.

A small cabin using 3 kWh per day in a 4.5 peak-sun-hour area might need:

3 kWh ÷ 4.5 sun hours × 1.25 = 0.83 kW solar array
830 watts ÷ 400W panels = about 3 panels

For off-grid use, panels are only one part of the system. You also need enough battery capacity for night and cloudy weather, plus an inverter that can handle simultaneous loads and startup surge from refrigerators, pumps, tools, and air conditioners.

Panel wattage changes the count

The same system size can use different numbers of panels depending on the panel watt rating. Higher-watt panels reduce the panel count, but they do not magically reduce the energy you need.

  • A 4 kW array needs about 10 panels at 400W each.
  • A 6 kW array needs about 15 panels at 400W each.
  • A 10 kW array needs about 25 panels at 400W each.
  • A 12 kW array needs about 30 panels at 400W each.

Residential panels often fall around 350W to 450W, while some larger panels are rated higher. Always compare panel count using the actual wattage of the panels you are considering.

Roof space and shade can be the real limit

After estimating panel count, check whether you have enough usable mounting space. Roofs with heavy shade, complex angles, vents, dormers, skylights, or poor orientation may not support the full array you calculated.

Ground mounts can solve some space and angle problems, but they add racking, trenching, wiring, permitting, and installation considerations. For RVs, vans, sheds, and small cabins, roof space often becomes the limiting factor quickly.

Do batteries change how many panels you need?

Batteries do not reduce the energy you need to produce. They change when you can use that energy. If you add batteries for backup or off-grid use, you may need extra solar capacity to recharge the batteries after overnight use or cloudy days.

For grid-tied homes, a battery may be optional. For cabins, RVs, sheds, and outage backup, battery sizing is usually just as important as panel sizing.

Common mistakes when estimating panel count

  • Ignoring system losses: panel ratings are lab numbers, not guaranteed daily output.
  • Using daylight hours instead of peak sun hours: ten hours of daylight is not ten full-power solar hours.
  • Forgetting seasonal changes: winter production can be much lower than summer production.
  • Only sizing panels: batteries, inverter, charge controller, wiring, fusing, disconnects, and mounting all matter.
  • Assuming one online answer fits every location: shade, utility rates, roof direction, and local rules change the math.
