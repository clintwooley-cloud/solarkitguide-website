How Much Solar Power Do I Need to Charge My EV?

To estimate solar for an electric vehicle, start with daily driving miles, multiply by the EV’s kWh per mile, add charging losses, then size extra solar panels around your average peak sun hours.

Quick answer

A typical EV driven 30–40 miles per day may use about 9–14 kWh of charging energy per day after losses. In a 4.5 peak-sun-hour area, that often means roughly 2.5–4 kW of additional solar, or about 7–10 extra 400-watt panels, to offset daily charging on an average day.

The basic EV solar formula

EV solar sizing is mostly an energy problem. You are not usually sending solar directly from the panels into the car. In most homes, solar offsets the energy used by a Level 2 charger through the home electrical system and utility meter.

Daily miles × EV kWh per mile = battery energy used
Battery energy × charging loss factor = wall energy needed
Wall energy ÷ peak sun hours × solar loss factor = extra solar array size

The EV efficiency number varies by vehicle, speed, temperature, tires, terrain, payload, and driving style. Charging losses also vary, but 10–15% is a reasonable starting estimate for many home Level 2 charging setups.

Example: efficient electric car

Suppose you drive an efficient EV around 35 miles per day and it averages 0.28 kWh per mile.

35 miles × 0.28 kWh/mi = 9.8 kWh used by the car
9.8 kWh × 1.15 charging loss = 11.3 kWh from the wall
11.3 kWh ÷ 4.5 sun hours × 1.25 solar losses = 3.1 kW solar array

With 400-watt panels, that is about eight additional panels. If your area gets less sun, you drive more miles, or winter production matters, the number can go higher.

Example: electric truck or large SUV

Electric trucks and larger SUVs often use more energy per mile than smaller EVs. A truck averaging 0.48 kWh per mile and driving 40 miles per day might need:

40 miles × 0.48 kWh/mi = 19.2 kWh used by the vehicle
19.2 kWh × 1.15 charging loss = 22.1 kWh from the wall
22.1 kWh ÷ 4.5 sun hours × 1.25 solar losses = 6.1 kW solar array

At 400 watts per panel, that is about 16 additional panels. This is why vehicle type matters. A large EV truck can require roughly twice the extra solar of a smaller commuter EV for the same daily mileage.

Common EV efficiency starting points

If you do not know your exact efficiency yet, use a conservative estimate and adjust after you have real charging data.

  • Efficient sedan: about 0.25–0.30 kWh per mile.
  • Compact crossover: about 0.28–0.34 kWh per mile.
  • Large SUV or performance EV: about 0.34–0.42 kWh per mile.
  • Electric pickup truck: about 0.45–0.60+ kWh per mile.
  • Cold weather or highway-heavy driving: use a higher number.

The best number is your own measured efficiency from the vehicle app or charger history, especially if your route includes high speeds, towing, mountains, or winter driving.

Do I need batteries to charge an EV with solar?

Not always. If your home is grid-tied, solar can offset EV charging through net metering or utility billing even if the car charges at night. In that setup, the grid acts like the timing bridge between daytime solar production and nighttime charging.

Batteries become more relevant if you want backup power, time-of-use rate shifting, or off-grid charging. But charging an EV from home batteries can require a very large battery bank, especially for trucks or long commutes.

Level 1 vs Level 2 charging

Solar array size is based on energy, not charger speed. A Level 2 charger does not necessarily use more total energy than Level 1 for the same miles, but it delivers that energy faster and usually requires a dedicated circuit.

  • Level 1: slower charging from a standard outlet; useful for short commutes but limited miles per night.
  • Level 2: faster home charging; common for EV owners and usually installed on a dedicated 240V circuit.
  • Solar offset: the panels offset the kWh used, while the charger circuit determines how quickly the vehicle charges.

A qualified electrician should size and install the EV charger circuit according to local code, panel capacity, breaker requirements, and the charger manufacturer’s specs.

Why your estimate may be different

The right solar size for EV charging can change based on:

  • Daily and weekly driving miles.
  • Vehicle efficiency in real conditions.
  • Charging loss and charger type.
  • Average peak sun hours in your area.
  • Winter production, shade, roof direction, and panel angle.
  • Whether you want to offset all charging or only part of it.
  • Utility rules, net metering, time-of-use rates, and interconnection limits.
