# 01-stop-buying-random-solar-kits

Stop buying random solar kits before you know what you actually need.

A solar kit that works great for one person may be completely wrong for your cabin, RV, shed, or backup power setup.

The big question is not “how many panels should I buy?”

The real question is: what are you trying to run, how many hours do you need it to run, and how much sunlight can you count on?

That decides your battery size, inverter size, solar panel wattage, and charge controller.

# 02-200-watt-kit-reality-check

A 200 watt solar kit is useful, but it is not magic.

A lot of people see a 200 watt starter kit and think it can run a whole cabin.

Usually, it cannot.

A 200 watt kit is better for smaller loads: charging batteries, lights, fans, phones, a small fridge in the right conditions, or learning how a basic solar setup works.

The real limit is not just the panel size. It is your battery capacity, your inverter, and how many watt-hours you use each day.

That is why I like 200 watt kits as a starter setup, not as a promise that they will run everything.

# 03-lithium-battery-charger-warning

If you buy a lithium battery for solar, do not assume your old charger is fine.

This is one of the easiest mistakes to make.

LiFePO4 batteries need a lithium-compatible charging profile.

That means your solar charge controller, AC wall charger, DC-to-DC charger, or RV converter needs to be set up for lithium or LiFePO4 charging.

A lead-acid-only charger may use the wrong voltage profile, may not fully charge the battery, or may behave in a way that is not ideal for the battery long term.

So before you upgrade to a 12 volt 100 amp-hour lithium battery, check the charger too.

# 04-solar-calculator-explainer

Here is the simple way to size a small solar setup.

First, list what you want to run.

Second, estimate how many watts each item uses.

Third, estimate how many hours per day you will use it.

Watts times hours equals watt-hours.

That number matters because batteries store energy in watt-hours, and solar panels replace those watt-hours when the sun is available.

Then you add some margin, because real life is not perfect. Clouds happen. Panels are not always at the perfect angle. Inverters waste some energy.

That is the basic idea behind the free calculator at SolarKitGuide.com.

It helps you think in loads, battery capacity, solar panel wattage, and inverter size — instead of just guessing.

If you are new to solar, start there before buying parts.

# 05-battery-before-panels

For small solar systems, your battery can matter more than your panels.

Panels collect energy, but the battery is what lets you use that energy later.

If your battery is too small, you may run out of power overnight even if you have enough panels during the day.

If your panels are too small, your battery may never fully recover after a cloudy day or heavy use.

The system has to balance.

For example, a 12 volt 100 amp-hour LiFePO4 battery is roughly around 1,280 watt-hours of stored energy.

That sounds like a lot until you start adding refrigerators, fans, lights, laptops, or inverters.

This is why I keep saying: calculate your loads first.

SolarKitGuide.com has a free calculator and beginner guides to help you match the battery, panels, inverter, and charger.

Do the math before buying the kit.

# 06-what-can-a-100ah-battery-run

What can a 12 volt 100 amp-hour lithium battery actually run?

A rough answer is this: it stores about 1,280 watt-hours.

So if you had a device using 100 watts, in a perfect world that battery could run it for about 12.8 hours.

But real life is not perfect.

If you are using an inverter, you lose some power in the conversion. You also may not want to plan around draining everything to the absolute limit.

So a 100Ah lithium battery is useful, but it is not unlimited.

It can be great for lights, fans, device charging, small DC loads, and some fridge or backup setups depending on the actual power draw.

The smart move is to list your loads and calculate watt-hours.

# 07-beginner-solar-mistakes

Here are three beginner solar mistakes I would avoid.

Number one: buying panels before calculating your loads.

Number two: buying a lithium battery without checking whether your charger supports LiFePO4.

Number three: sizing your inverter for one appliance, but forgetting what may run at the same time.

Solar is not just panels.

It is panels, batteries, charge controllers, inverters, wiring, fuses, and the actual stuff you want to power.

If those parts do not match, the system will disappoint you.

That is why SolarKitGuide.com is built around simple calculators and beginner-friendly guides.

Use the calculator, read the basics, and then look at equipment.

It is a lot cheaper to fix the plan than to fix the wrong purchase.

# 08-cabin-solar-starter

If you are planning solar for a small cabin, start small on paper before you start spending money.

Write down the basics.

Lights.

Phone charging.

Wi-Fi or a router if you need it.

A fan.

A small refrigerator.

Maybe a water pump.

Then estimate how long each one runs per day.

That gives you a daily energy target.

From there, you can estimate battery capacity, panel wattage, inverter size, and how much margin you need for cloudy days.

A cabin system can be simple, but guessing can get expensive fast.

SolarKitGuide.com has a free solar calculator and beginner guides to help you plan before you buy.

Start with the loads, not the panels.
