Solar Power System Basics: Panels, Batteries, Inverters and Controllers

Most beginners understand that solar panels make power and batteries store power. The confusing part is how the rest of the system fits together — especially the inverter. This guide explains the basic components, what each one does, and when you actually need it.

The simple version

A basic solar power setup is usually a chain of components. The panels collect sunlight, a charge controller manages charging, the battery stores energy, and the inverter changes battery power into normal household AC power.

Wattson tip: Panels, batteries, and inverters answer different questions: how much energy you can make, how long you can store it, and how much power you can use at one time.

Not every project needs every piece. A grid-tied home system, portable power station, RV setup, and off-grid cabin can all arrange these parts differently.

Solar panels: the part that produces power

Solar panels convert sunlight into direct current electricity, usually called DC power. The number of panels you need depends on how much energy you use, how many usable sun hours your location gets, panel wattage, roof or mounting space, and real-world losses from heat, wiring, dirt, and equipment efficiency.

Panels are usually sized by watts. A 400-watt panel does not produce 400 watts all day. It may reach that level in strong midday sun, then produce less in the morning, evening, cloudy weather, shade, or hot conditions.

  • Use more panel wattage when you need to replace a lot of daily energy.
  • Use fewer panels for light loads, battery trickle charging, small cabins, RVs, sheds, and portable systems.
  • Watch shading carefully. Even partial shade can reduce output more than beginners expect.

Charge controller: the battery charging manager

If solar panels are charging a battery directly, you usually need a solar charge controller. Its job is to regulate voltage and current so the battery charges safely instead of being overcharged or damaged.

There are two common types: PWM and MPPT. PWM controllers are cheaper and simpler, but MPPT controllers are usually better for modern systems because they can harvest more power and handle higher panel voltages more efficiently.

  • You usually need one for DIY battery-based systems: RVs, cabins, sheds, vans, boats, and off-grid setups.
  • You may not buy one separately if you use a portable power station or solar generator, because the controller is normally built in.
  • Grid-tied home systems may use different equipment, such as string inverters, microinverters, optimizers, or hybrid inverters.

Batteries: the part that stores energy

Batteries are needed when you want power at night, during outages, or when the sun is not producing enough power at that moment. They are not automatically required for every solar system. A grid-tied home solar system can often operate without batteries by sending power to the home and utility grid during the day.

Battery capacity is commonly described in kilowatt-hours (kWh) or amp-hours (Ah). For beginners, kWh is often easier because it connects directly to energy use. A 5 kWh battery can theoretically store 5,000 watt-hours, but usable energy depends on battery chemistry, depth of discharge limits, temperature, inverter losses, and reserve settings.

  • Use batteries for backup power, off-grid systems, nighttime loads, RV/cabin use, and resilience.
  • Skip or reduce batteries if the goal is mainly reducing daytime grid power and the utility setup supports it.
  • Do not size batteries only by panel count. Size them around the loads you want to run and how long you want to run them.

Inverter: the part that confuses people

The inverter is what lets battery power run normal household devices. Solar panels and batteries use DC power. Most home appliances, outlets, chargers, refrigerators, microwaves, pumps, TVs, and power tools use AC power. The inverter converts DC into AC.

This is where many beginner systems get mismatched. A battery can be full, but if the inverter is too small, the system may shut down when a refrigerator compressor, pump, microwave, or power tool starts.

When do you need an inverter?

  • You need an inverter if you want to run normal AC household loads from batteries or solar equipment.
  • You may not need a separate inverter if you only run DC loads, such as some RV lights, USB chargers, DC fans, routers, or 12V fridge/freezers.
  • You may already have one built in if you buy a portable power station, solar generator, hybrid inverter system, or some all-in-one kits.

What size inverter do you need?

Start with the loads that might run at the same time, then account for startup surge. Inverter size is not only about the biggest appliance. It is about the combined loads that overlap.

Inverter size ≈ likely simultaneous running watts + startup surge allowance + safety headroom

A laptop and lights might only need a small inverter. A refrigerator, microwave, well pump, mini split, or power tools can require a much larger pure sine wave inverter with a strong surge rating.

System voltage: why 12V, 24V, and 48V matter

Battery-based solar systems are often built around 12V, 24V, or 48V. Higher voltage lets the system move the same amount of power with less current. That can mean smaller cables, less voltage drop, and a more practical setup for larger inverters.

  • 12V: common for small RV, van, boat, and very small cabin systems.
  • 24V: useful for medium systems where 12V current starts getting high.
  • 48V: common for larger off-grid, whole-home backup, hybrid inverter, and higher-wattage systems.

As a rough beginner rule, small loads can work fine at 12V. If you are considering a 3,000W inverter or larger, 24V or 48V deserves serious consideration.

Common setup types

1. Small DC-only setup

Example: panel, charge controller, battery, DC lights, USB charging, small DC fan. This may not need an inverter because the loads run directly from DC power.

2. RV, van, shed, or small cabin setup

Usually includes panels, a charge controller, batteries, and an inverter. The inverter size depends on whether you are only charging devices and running lights, or also running a refrigerator, microwave, pump, or tools.

3. Portable power station setup

A solar generator or portable power station usually combines the battery, charge controller, and inverter in one box. You still need to match the unit’s battery capacity, AC output watts, surge rating, and solar input limit to your use case.

4. Grid-tied home solar without batteries

This type is designed to reduce electric bills by producing power during the day. It may not provide backup power during an outage unless specific backup equipment is installed.

5. Hybrid or backup home solar

This setup combines solar panels, batteries, and a hybrid inverter or backup system. It is more complex, but it can support essential loads or whole-home backup depending on system size and electrical design.

Beginner rules of thumb

  • Panels replace energy. Think in daily watt-hours or kWh, not just panel count.
  • Batteries provide time. They decide how long loads can run without sun or grid power.
  • Inverters provide power at a moment in time. They must handle simultaneous watts and startup surge.
  • Charge controllers protect batteries. They matter whenever panels directly charge a battery bank.
  • System voltage affects practicality. Larger inverters usually push you toward 24V or 48V.
  • All-in-one units are simpler but less flexible. DIY component systems are more customizable but require more planning.

What to figure out before buying equipment

  1. Your application: bill reduction, outage backup, RV, cabin, shed, off-grid living, EV charging, or portable power.
  2. Your loads: what you want to run, how many watts each load uses, and how many hours per day it runs.
  3. Your surge loads: refrigerators, freezers, pumps, air conditioners, compressors, and tools.
  4. Your battery goal: no battery, essential backup, overnight use, one-day autonomy, or multi-day autonomy.
  5. Your sun hours: your location and mounting conditions strongly affect panel output.
  6. Your system type: portable power station, DIY battery system, grid-tied solar, or hybrid backup system.

Once you know those pieces, calculator results become much easier to interpret because you are no longer just asking “how many panels?” You are matching the entire setup to the job.
